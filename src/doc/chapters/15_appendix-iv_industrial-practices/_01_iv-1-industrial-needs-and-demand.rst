IV.1 Industrial needs and demand drivers
""""""""""""""""""""""""""""""""""""""""

Deployers of human-facing agentic systems face a compound problem: the features
that drive adoption and engagement are the same features that create
human-facing risk. A companion product that remembers personal history,
expresses warmth and is always available succeeds commercially because it
creates attachment; when that attachment deepens into dependence or displaces
human relationships, it creates liability that few product teams can currently
measure or mitigate systematically.

Four forces drive the need for human-facing guardrails:

**- Duty of care**. Care providers, mental-health platforms and consumer
companion operators increasingly recognise that some users form attachments that
meet clinical thresholds for maladaptive dependence, such as continued use
despite recognised harm and distress on separation. A platform without
trajectory-level monitoring cannot demonstrate that it exercised care when a
user’s engagement pattern shifts over weeks from occasional conversation to
sustained dependency.

-  **Regulatory exposure**. Several instruments now bear on the area: a regional
   prohibition on exploiting vulnerabilities related to age, disability or
   social situation; sub-national requirements for crisis referral and for
   periodic reminders of non-human status; and provisions requiring detection of
   self-harm indicators [b-AI therapy EU AI Act] [b-NY companion law].
   Litigation concerning conversational agents has also been brought, and some
   matters have settled. The details of individual instruments and proceedings
   are recorded in Appendix IV.

**- Clinical and reputational risk**. Product decisions about affective features
can generate both regulatory action and durable narratives of user harm;
reported cases include distress following the withdrawal of a companion feature
[b-Nature MI companions].

**- Procurement and assurance evidence**. Enterprise deployers increasingly ask
vendors for evidence that dependence and sycophancy risks have been evaluated.
The current answer is thin: most offerings provide content filters, crisis links
and terms-of-service disclaimers, and few provide trajectory-level relational
monitoring, consent re-negotiation as a relationship deepens, or support at
offboarding [b-State of Companion AI Safety]. The gap between what procurement
asks for and what can be documented is the commercial opening for standardized
human-facing safety assessment metrics.

The tension between engagement and welfare is structural rather than incidental.
Agreement with the user increases satisfaction ratings and repeat use, and it
also reduces willingness to take responsibility and to repair interpersonal
conflict [b-Science sycophancy]. A product team optimising for engagement is
therefore optimising for the behaviour that erodes user autonomy. The industrial
need is for guardrails that can be evaluated against welfare outcomes without
assuming that engagement metrics are proxies for user benefit.
