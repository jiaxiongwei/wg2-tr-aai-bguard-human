Appendix II Dimensions of human impact for agentic AI assessment
================================================================

This appendix sets out the dimensions along which the effects of agentic AI on
humans are assessed. Each dimension identifies an aspect of human experience and
social organisation that an assessment records in a given setting. The
dimensions support structured observation: an assessor records what is present,
absent, salient or variable in the setting under assessment. The dimensions are
descriptive categories for recording observations, not criteria for judging
settings, traditions or societies.

II.1 Ethical dimension
----------------------

The ethical outlooks that shape expectations about when an autonomous agent may
act and what counts as a harmful outcome. These outlooks inform thresholds for
action, prohibitions, and the weighting of outcomes, rights, character and
relationships. Different ethical traditions set different thresholds for action
and different accounts of harm. Consequentialist approaches weigh aggregated
outcomes; deontological approaches emphasise duties and rights; virtue
approaches focus on character and manner; relational and communal outlooks
emphasise role-based propriety and collective flourishing; religious frameworks
may protect specified essential interests. Multiple outlooks may overlap in a
single deployment setting.

The assessor records which ethical expectations are expressed or encoded in the
setting, how thresholds for action are described, and which accounts of harm are
treated as relevant.

II.2 Cultural dimension
-----------------------

The cultural values and norms that shape expectations about delegation,
authority, transparency, time horizons and interaction style. These values
operate at both collective and individual levels. Expectations differ regarding
whether an agent acts for an individual or a group, how much deference an agent
shows, how much ambiguity is tolerated, whether relationships or immediate
outcomes are prioritised, and how information is shared. Individual-level
cultural traits may differ from aggregate patterns. The same agent behaviour may
meet expectations in one setting and not in another.

The assessor records the cultural expectations that are salient in the setting,
the interaction styles that are treated as appropriate, and the level at which
those expectations are expressed.

II.3 Religious dimension
------------------------

Religious frameworks and sensitivities that shape what an agent may do, what it
may display, and which interests are treated as essential. These frameworks may
inform prohibitions, priorities and the interpretation of harm. Religious
traditions differ in the interests they protect, the prohibitions they maintain,
the transactions they restrict, and the weight they give to indirect and
cumulative effects. Some traditions emphasise principled conformity, others
combine principled and consequential elements. Sensitivity to religious content,
timing and imagery varies across settings.

The assessor records the religious considerations that are expressed in the
setting, the interests or prohibitions that are identified as relevant, and the
ways in which those considerations are described by participants.

II.4 Social and relational dimension
------------------------------------

The social relationships, roles and communal bonds that an agent may affect.
This dimension covers role-based expectations, hierarchy, face, family and
community ties, and collective welfare. Expectations differ regarding whether
users are treated identically or according to role, whether deference is
expected, whether loss of face is a harm, and whether communal or
intergenerational obligations are salient. Some settings treat individual
autonomy as exercised within communal obligation; others treat it as exercised
independently. Consultation with community representatives may be relevant in
some settings and not in others.

The assessor records the relational expectations that are present, the roles and
bonds that are identified as relevant, and the ways in which communal or
relational effects are described.

II.5 Cognitive and psychological dimension
------------------------------------------

The cognitive and psychological effects of agent behaviour on users, including
trust, confidence, reliance, perceived competence, perceived humility, and the
cultivation or corrosion of trust. Baseline trust in automated systems differs
across settings and individuals. Users differ in their tolerance for ambiguity,
their preference for explicit rules or exploratory behaviour, and their
expectations about agent assertiveness. The manner of interaction may be
experienced as supportive in one setting and as overreaching in another.

The assessor records the cognitive and psychological responses that are observed
or reported, the expectations about trust and reliance that are expressed, and
the interaction manners that are treated as appropriate.

II.6 Linguistic dimension
-------------------------

Language, idiom and context sensitivity in agent interaction. This dimension
covers deferential or direct language, hierarchy marking, and the sharing of
information within or across groups. Language use that is appropriate in one
setting may be inappropriate in another. Some settings expect deferential
language and acknowledgement of hierarchy; others expect direct language and
treatment of users as peers. Information-sharing norms differ, with some
settings treating information as communal and others treating each interaction
as confidential.

The assessor records the language and idiom expectations that are salient, the
interaction styles that are used or expected, and the information-sharing norms
that are expressed.

II.7 Legal and regulatory dimension
-----------------------------------

The legal and regulatory expectations that apply to agent behaviour in a
setting, including compliance requirements, procedural rights, content
governance, and impact assessment expectations. Legal and regulatory
expectations differ across jurisdictions and sectors. Some settings emphasise
fundamental-rights impact assessment, others sector-specific compliance, content
governance, inclusive-design requirements, procedural rights, strategic
alignment or developmental contribution. These expectations may be expressed as
prohibitions, procedural requirements or outcome requirements.

The assessor records the legal and regulatory expectations that are identified
in the setting, the instruments or processes through which they are expressed,
and the compliance mechanisms that are present.

II.8 Age and developmental dimension
------------------------------------

The age and developmental status of users, and the expectations that attach to
age of majority, child protection, and developmental vulnerability. Settings
differ in the age at which users are treated as adults, the protections that
apply to children and young people, and the expectations about agent behaviour
toward different age groups. Developmental considerations may affect what counts
as harm, what information may be shared, and what forms of interaction are
treated as appropriate.

The assessor records the age-related expectations that are present, the
protections that apply to different age groups, and the ways in which
developmental status is described in the setting.

II.9 Access and economic dimension
----------------------------------

The distribution of access to agentic AI and its economic effects on users,
including cost, availability, digital literacy, and distributional consequences.
Settings differ in who has access to agentic systems, the cost of access, the
digital literacy required, and the distribution of benefits and burdens.
Distributional concerns may be prominent in some settings and less salient in
others. Access may be mediated by infrastructure, language or platform
availability.

The assessor records the access conditions that are observed, the economic
effects that are identified, and the distributional patterns that are described.

II.10 Governance and observability dimension
--------------------------------------------

The governance arrangements and observability mechanisms through which agent
behaviour is monitored, explained, audited and contested. Settings differ in the
transparency of agent behaviour, the availability of explanations, the presence
of audit trails, the mechanisms for contestation, and the distribution of
responsibility among developers, deployers and users. Monitoring may be treated
as acceptable in some settings and as intrusive in others.

The assessor records the governance and observability arrangements that are
present, the mechanisms for explanation and contestation, and the expectations
about monitoring that are expressed.

II.11 Cross-cutting observation dimension
-----------------------------------------

Observations that span multiple dimensions and that are not captured within a
single dimension. This dimension accommodates interactions between dimensions
and setting-specific factors. The salience and interaction of dimensions differ
across settings. A factor that is central in one setting may be peripheral in
another. New or unanticipated factors may emerge in a given deployment context.

The assessor records observations that cross dimension boundaries, notes
interactions between dimensions, and identifies setting-specific factors that
are not captured elsewhere.

The dimensions in this appendix are used to record what is observed in a
setting. The record makes no ranking of settings, traditions or societies. The
dimensions complement the categorised assessment factors of clause 9.2 and the
dimensions of variation listed in clause 9.8. Together they support a structured
account of the human effects of agentic AI in the setting under assessment.
