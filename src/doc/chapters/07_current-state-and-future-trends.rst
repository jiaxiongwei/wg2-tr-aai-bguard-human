Current state and future trends
===============================

Current state
-------------

Human-facing agentic systems are now deployed at scale, and the features that
drive their adoption are the same features that create human-facing risk. This
clause summarises the current state; the detailed survey is given in Appendix
IV.

The demand for human-facing guardrails comes from four converging forces:

-  a duty of care towards users who form sustained attachments;

-  regulatory exposure under instruments that now bear on companion-style and
   affective systems;

-  clinical and reputational risk where dependence, distress or crisis response
   are implicated; and

-  procurement and assurance evidence required by enterprise deployers.

Industrial practice remains fragmented. The mechanisms most widely documented
are disclosure of non-human identity, a reliable crisis response path, a stated
stance on emotional companionship, sycophancy mitigation, age or usage measures,
and published evaluation of human-facing risk. Three patterns are observable:
detection has moved from single-turn filters to multi-turn trajectory
monitoring; supervisory components are interposed between the user and the
model; and cognitive safety kernels are embedded at the application layer.

Two gaps recur: stated safeguards are not systematically verifiable by external
parties, and documentation of practice is uneven across providers.

The current state is therefore one of converging demand and uneven supply. What
is needed is not another statement of principle but a shared way to describe,
collect and assess the evidence that a human-facing safeguard is present and
effective. That is the purpose of the assessment framework in clause 9.

Future trends
-------------

Three trajectories are anticipated over the next three to five years. First,
regulatory consolidation and specificity: instruments that currently address
affective systems indirectly are expected to name the mechanisms they require,
and to attach evidence obligations to them. Second, measurement infrastructure
and certification: independent evaluation, cohort baselines and third-party
conformity assessment are expected to move from exception to routine for systems
with large human-facing populations. Third, architectural intervention beyond
filtering: control is expected to shift further towards supervision, memory and
intervention layers, because output filtering alone cannot address cumulative
harm.

These trajectories raise the same questions the current state leaves open: what
is to be verified, at which layer, by whom, and against which baseline. The
assessment framework in clause 9 is written to be usable at each stage of that
shift, and to remain applicable as regulatory and technical expectations change.

The evidence base for both the current state and these trends is collected in
Appendix IV.
